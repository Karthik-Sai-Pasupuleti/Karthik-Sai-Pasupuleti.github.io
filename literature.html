Key Findings

What the Evidence Says

A standout result from the corpus: addressing local firing saturation lets a spiking detector match — and on event data beat — far hungrier ANN models.

Architecture

Local firing saturation

Adjacent neurons saturating at max firing rate — caused by variance accumulation in membrane shortcut connections — destroys feature discriminability and wastes energy. Identified as a fundamental SNN-detector problem.

Efficiency

52.2% AP at 40.4 mJ

SpikeDet beats the previous best SNN detector (SpikeYOLO-L: 48.9% AP, 84.2 mJ) by +3.3% AP at half the power — and matches ANN YOLOv12-M (52.5%) at 3.8× the energy efficiency on COCO 2017.

Accuracy

Beating ANNs on event data

On the GEN1 event-camera dataset, SpikeDet (47.6% AP, 9.9 mJ) surpasses ANN detector RVT (47.2% AP, 116.7 mJ) — the first SNN to outperform ANNs here, at an order-of-magnitude lower power.

Manuscript

Review Outline

The structure of the systematic review paper currently in preparation.

1

Introduction

Autonomous-driving perception challenges, motivation for SNNs, key advantages (energy, temporal processing) and current limitations within neuromorphic computing.

2

Background on Spiking Neural Networks

Neuron models (LIF, IF), temporal dynamics, learning rules, event-driven processing, and comparison with ANNs.

3

SNN Object-Detection Architectures

Direct-training vs ANN-to-SNN conversion; YOLO variants and spiking transformers; architectural trade-offs.

4

SNNs for Autonomous-Driving Applications

Event cameras and LiDAR, multi-sensor fusion, real-time constraints and edge deployment.

5

Energy Efficiency & Performance Metrics

Energy/latency metrics, accuracy–efficiency trade-offs, and comparison tables across benchmark datasets.

6

Research Gaps & Future Directions

Standardised benchmarks, unified energy measurement, and the path to deployable spiking perception.
